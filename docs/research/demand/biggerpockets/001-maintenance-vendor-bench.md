## Post-By-Post Notes

### 1. Reliable general maintenance vendors in Detroit

Evidence value: High.

This thread is useful because it comes from a property-management company that already has a vendor list but needs to expand as it grows. The request is not "we have no vendors." It is "our existing list is not enough coverage for the next stage of operations."

Key signals:

- The PM company specializes in single-family homes.
- They already have a good vendor list, but growth creates pressure to expand it.
- They want enough work for everyone without overwhelming the current team.
- Target work includes sub-$1,000 tenant work orders, basic plumbing/electrical repairs, drywall patching, painting, appliance installs/troubleshooting, minor carpentry, and city/compliance inspection deficiency lists.
- The thread produced little discussion, but the job category list is valuable.

Interpretation:

Vendor coverage is a scaling problem. A PM can be operationally healthy at one portfolio size and under-covered at the next. The relevant vendor is not a full GC for giant projects; it is often a general maintenance operator who can handle small tenant work orders and compliance punch lists.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should support a vendor coverage map by:

- property type
- geography
- trade/category
- small-work-order ability
- compliance punch-list experience
- current workload/capacity
- backup/overflow readiness

### 2. Build Your Vendor Army

Evidence value: Medium.

This post was more advice/content than discussion, but it aligns strongly with the emergency-readiness theme.

Key signals:

- Emergency maintenance costs and response delays spike during holidays, weather swings, and peak seasons.
- Teams with vendor relationships before the emergency get better response.
- Suggested vendor bench includes HVAC, electricians, plumbers, and handymen.
- Questions to ask vendors include service-area response time, best days/times, weekend rules, fees, and preferred request process.
- The core advice is to build relationships during quiet periods rather than searching during a 2 a.m. emergency.

Interpretation:

Emergency vendor readiness is relationship inventory. Availability during emergencies is not a generic attribute; it depends on prior relationship, vendor preference, timing, and request channel.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should track emergency-readiness fields:

- emergency availability
- after-hours rules
- weekend/holiday policy
- expected response time by area
- preferred request method
- relationship strength
- last emergency success/failure

### 3. How PM companies manage maintenance and repairs

Evidence value: Very high.

This thread is highly aligned with VendorRadar because it frames maintenance process as a PM selection criterion for owners.

Key signals:

- Tenant satisfaction is tied to maintenance speed and effectiveness.
- Owners should ask how a PM tracks incoming maintenance requests and subsequent work orders.
- Software should prevent requests from falling through the cracks.
- Online tenant maintenance requests are expected.
- After-hours emergencies should be handled by someone qualified, not an answering machine.
- Preventative maintenance protects property value when owners are willing to approve necessary repairs.
- Owners should ask whether the PM uses in-house maintenance or preferred vendors.
- Vendor questions include workers comp, licensing, bonding, insurance, 24/7 availability, and whether the PM oversees quality, code compliance, and cost effectiveness.
- Occupied-unit entry policies matter because contractor access creates risk.
- Itemized statements and receipts add accountability and transparency.
- Tenant-performed repairs and tenant-hired handymen create liability/quality risk.
- Turnover process should include cleaning, painting, re-keying, inspections, and proactive improvement suggestions.
- A reply described pain points around single intake, lost texts/emails, vendor coordination, cost records, and nudges when work stalls.
- Another reply asked how PMs document property condition and repairs to hold tenants accountable.

Interpretation:

Maintenance management is the owner-facing surface of PM competence. Owners judge the PM not only by rent collection, but by request intake, vendor selection, emergency response, compliance, proof, cost transparency, and tenant satisfaction.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar can support PM credibility by producing a structured maintenance process layer:

- tenant request intake
- work-order status
- qualified vendor assignment
- occupied-entry policy
- proof capture
- receipt/invoice attachment
- owner approval
- code/compliance flags
- turnover checklist
- tenant accountability evidence

### 5. Default authorization limits in Midwest

Evidence value: Very high.

This thread gives the strongest early BiggerPockets signal on approval thresholds.

Key signals:

- Remote landlord asks what maintenance amounts should require owner approval across life-safety, habitability, routine, and cosmetic/deferrable repairs.
- Life-safety emergencies should generally have no normal approval limit.
- Routine repair thresholds discussed were commonly in the few-hundred-dollar range.
- Some owners use a low routine threshold so vendors can fix small items while onsite.
- Local cost conditions matter: one market may make a low threshold useless because nearly nothing can be completed under it.
- PM-side comments emphasized notification timing, transparency, cost breakdowns, before/after pictures/videos, and proactive communication.
- Owners complain when they only learn about repairs after seeing monthly statements.
- Proof via photos/videos was framed as a major owner-confidence tool.

Interpretation:

Authorization limits are not just financial controls. They are the trust contract between remote owner, PM, vendor, and tenant. The threshold has to balance response speed, tenant safety, owner control, local costs, and repair category.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should model approval rules as structured policy:

- routine auto-approval limit
- emergency override
- habitability rules
- life-safety escalation
- quote-required threshold
- proof-required threshold
- owner-notification timing
- after-the-fact reporting deadline

## Cross-Thread Patterns

### 1. Vendor Bench Is Better Language Than Vendor List

"Vendor list" sounds static. "Vendor bench" captures redundancy, availability, trade coverage, and emergency readiness.

### 2. Small Work Orders Are A Distinct Vendor Category

Sub-$1,000 tenant work orders require a different vendor profile than renovations, restorations, or specialty trades. PMs need vendors who can handle recurring small jobs without turning each one into a major project.

### 3. Maintenance Is A PM Trust Surface

Owners evaluate PMs through maintenance experience: intake, communication, cost control, vendor quality, approvals, receipts, and proof.

### 4. Compliance Shows Up In Owner Language Too

BiggerPockets owners explicitly ask whether contractors are licensed, bonded, insured, and covered by workers comp. This validates the Reddit compliance findings from another audience.

### 5. Authorization Limits Are Workflow Design

Approval thresholds should vary by repair type and urgency. A single flat number is easy to understand, but may fail for emergencies, habitability, and local cost realities.
