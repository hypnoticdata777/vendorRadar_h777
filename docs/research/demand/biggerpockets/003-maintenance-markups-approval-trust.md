## Executive Summary

BiggerPockets Search 3 is the clearest owner/PM money-trust batch so far.

The strongest finding:

```text
Maintenance markups are not automatically the problem. Hidden economics are the problem.
```

Owners often react negatively to maintenance markups because they fear misaligned incentives: unnecessary repairs, inflated vendor invoices, undisclosed discounts, kickbacks, or PM-owned maintenance companies billing through a different name. PM-side commenters defend markups as legitimate compensation for coordination labor, vendor network building, tenant scheduling, bid review, supervision, quality control, warranty follow-up, and cash-flow risk.

The real product opportunity is not to decide whether markups are good or bad. It is to make the economics, approval rules, repair category, vendor invoice, owner authorization, and proof trail visible enough that both sides can trust the process.

## Post-By-Post Notes

### 1. Property Manager Charges 10% markup for all service calls

Evidence value: Very high.

This thread starts with a common owner question: if I already pay a management fee, why is there also a markup on service calls?

Key signals:

- A 10% service-call markup was described by several commenters as common if disclosed.
- Owners may accept the fee if the PM is otherwise good and the fee is visible.
- PM-side defense: the markup compensates the manager for acting partly like a GC/coordinator, especially when properties have repair-heavy histories.
- Investor-side pushback: arranging repairs feels like part of the monthly management fee.
- Commenters framed the choice as a fee model question: lower monthly management fee plus activity-based maintenance markup versus higher all-inclusive management fee.
- The owner discovered that the markup applied broadly to service-related items, not only major repairs, which changed how they perceived the fee.

Interpretation:

The owner pain is partly expectation mismatch. A fee disclosed in the contract can still feel surprising if the owner did not understand how frequently it applies.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should distinguish:

- fee disclosed
- fee understood
- fee triggered
- fee justified by work category
- fee connected to proof/coordination evidence

### 2. Property Management - Repair Mark-ups?

Evidence value: Extremely high.

This thread is one of the strongest in the BiggerPockets batch because it exposes the difference between disclosed markup and undisclosed vendor-discount capture.

Key signals:

- Owner discovered the PM may be earning money from repair/vendor arrangements not clearly shown in the contract.
- A repair example triggered the owner's concern when the PM resisted sharing the underlying vendor invoice.
- PM-side commenters argued that 10-15% markups can be reasonable because maintenance coordination creates real overhead.
- A transparent PM commenter described including the original vendor invoice in the owner portal and spelling out the markup contractually.
- Several commenters considered undisclosed markup or hidden discount retention a major red flag.
- One owner noted that repair markups can become as material as, or larger than, the monthly management fee in repair-heavy years.
- There is a conflict-of-interest concern: if the PM earns a percentage of repairs, higher repair volume or higher repair costs may become financially attractive.
- Multiple comments distinguished between "markup after negotiated discount but still below retail" and "PM pockets savings while charging owner market rate."

Interpretation:

Repair markup is acceptable to many owners when it is disclosed, capped or reasonable, tied to real coordination work, and accompanied by actual vendor invoices. It becomes toxic when hidden, vague, or disconnected from proof.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should preserve a transparent cost stack:

- vendor invoice amount
- PM markup percentage or flat coordination fee
- discount, if disclosed
- owner-facing total
- approval threshold status
- proof packet status
- management agreement rule that authorized the charge

### 3. Do maintenance Markups by Property Managers Make Sense?

Evidence value: Very high.

This PM-authored thread gives the strongest defense of markups and the cleanest category model.

Key signals:

- PM-side argument: maintenance coordination includes sourcing vendors, negotiating pricing, scheduling tenants, ensuring access, following up, quality control, managing rework, and sometimes fronting costs.
- Markups can tie compensation to actual maintenance workload instead of increasing base management fees for everyone.
- Volume discounts may allow owners to pay less than retail even after a markup.
- A PM commenter proposed three categories:
  - small maintenance under a threshold: actual cost, no markup
  - repairs above the maintenance limit: actual cost plus markup
  - renovations/large rent-ready work: higher markup because coordination is more complex
- Owner-side counterpoint: low-maintenance properties may not require much coordination, and automated tenant-to-vendor workflows reduce PM labor.
- Another counterpoint: in-house maintenance or hourly charges can hide markup inside labor rates even when PMs claim not to mark up vendor invoices.
- Reliable vendors remain hard to find and keep; this supports the argument that vendor networks have value.

Interpretation:

The best model is not "markup or no markup." It is category-based compensation matched to actual coordination load.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should support maintenance categories:

```text
maintenance | repair | renovation | emergency | owner-directed | warranty | turnover
```

Each category can have different approval rules, markup/fee rules, proof requirements, and bid requirements.

### 4. Managing my manager - how to best approach maintenance requests

Evidence value: Extremely high.

This thread combines owner education, maintenance markup, bid reasonableness, repair approval, and PM process expectations.

Key signals:

- Owner with small multifamily properties is asked to approve expensive maintenance estimates but lacks confidence to judge if prices are reasonable.
- The PM charges a 15% maintenance markup.
- Commenters warned that 15% may be high for routine jobs and less justifiable for low-coordination work such as simple testing.
- Markups may be more defensible for larger projects needing coordination, supervision, or inspection after completion.
- One response suggests comparing annual total cost, not only monthly management percentage.
- Owners should read and understand the PM agreement, fee schedule, termination terms, lease process, maintenance process, turnover process, and eviction process.
- Professional PMs should be able to explain their processes clearly and may have written SOPs.
- Multiple bids are useful for larger or less urgent jobs, but may be unrealistic or harmful for urgent issues.
- Owners should periodically shop or benchmark repair pricing to verify fair procurement.

Interpretation:

Owners do not merely want cheaper repairs. They want enough structure to know when they should trust, approve, question, or compare.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should provide "repair reasonableness context":

- repair category
- urgency
- estimate amount
- approval threshold
- whether multiple bids are expected
- comparable past jobs
- vendor history
- owner approval status
- whether PM markup is justified by coordination category

### 5. Property Mgr. percentage of repairs?

Evidence value: Very high.

This thread directly asks whether a 15% repair percentage creates misaligned incentives.

Key signals:

- Some PMs avoid maintenance markups because they view them as a conflict of interest.
- Other commenters say markups are common, often around 8-12%, with 15% on the upper end in some markets.
- Approval threshold was proposed as the main owner control.
- Commenters described multiple hidden markup models:
  - in-house staff billed to owner at higher hourly rate than true cost
  - PM-owned maintenance company
  - contractor kickbacks
  - disclosed hybrid markup
- A PM-side commenter described no markup under a small threshold, 10% for mid-size repairs, and 20% for larger renovations.
- Another investor said for big-ticket repairs, owners should obtain at least one competing bid themselves.

Interpretation:

The repair markup debate is inseparable from incentive design. Owners fear paying PMs to create more repair volume; PMs argue they need compensation for work that owners underestimate.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar can reduce conflict by tying fee rules to visible thresholds and proof, not vague trust.

### 6. What does your property management company charge you?

Evidence value: High.

This benchmark thread shows that owners compare fee stacks poorly unless all fees are visible.

Key signals:

- Monthly management fees often appear around the high single digits to low double digits, but the actual total cost depends on leasing fees, renewal fees, maintenance markups, trip fees, retainers/reserves, vacancy, and other charges.
- Owners may incorrectly assume paying contractors directly prevents PM compensation from occurring behind the scenes.
- There are concerns about PMs using related-party vendors, spouse-owned vendors, or opaque outside companies.
- Trust accounts/reserves are used for maintenance handling.
- Some owners prefer direct contractor payment and no maintenance markup.
- Others tolerate fees if the PM service is good and switching would be painful.

Interpretation:

PM pricing is a stack, not a single percentage. Owners get surprised when the full stack is not modeled before signing.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar could include PM/vendor fee comparison logic later:

- base management fee
- leasing fee
- renewal fee
- maintenance markup
- coordination fee
- quote request fee
- reserve requirement
- transfer/termination fees
- hidden/related-party vendor risk flags

### 7. PM Fees - Too Much or Normal?

Evidence value: High.

This thread shows fee-stack complexity and how many small fees create owner anxiety.

Key signals:

- The fee list included management fee, leasing fee, renewal fee, onboarding fee, annual admin fee, maintenance reserve, approval limits, emergency limits, home warranty claim fee, multiple quote request fee, filter supply fee, repair/improvement markup, late/administrative fees, insurance documentation penalties, optional protection programs, termination fees, vacant property termination fee, and file transfer fee.
- Some local commenters said many fees were normal for the market.
- Others flagged specific fees as high or philosophically misaligned.
- Termination/transfer fees generated concern.
- One commenter warned that paying more does not guarantee better PM service.
- Home warranty involvement was flagged as a possible repair-delay source.

Interpretation:

Owners need pricing explainability before trust can form. A long fee schedule is not automatically wrong, but it must be legible, justified, and aligned with owner goals.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar's owner-facing outputs should explain why a maintenance charge happened, not merely display the charge. For PM evaluation tools, total cost modeling matters more than headline management fee.

## Cross-Thread Patterns

### 1. Markups Need Context

A 10% or 15% markup is interpreted differently depending on:

- whether it is disclosed
- whether original vendor invoices are shown
- whether PM received a discount
- whether the job required coordination
- whether the property is old or maintenance-heavy
- whether the PM has in-house staff
- whether there is a cap
- whether the owner has approval rights

### 2. Approval Thresholds Are The Main Control Surface

Repair thresholds repeatedly show up as the mechanism that balances speed and owner control.

Common structure:

```text
small routine work: auto-approve
mid-size repair: owner approval
large repair/renovation: multiple bids when time allows
emergency/life-safety: immediate action with after-the-fact reporting
```

### 3. Three Quotes Are Useful But Not Always Rational

Owners want bids to prevent overcharging. PMs warn that bids cost time, delay tenant resolution, exhaust contractors, and are unrealistic for urgent/small jobs.

Product rule:

```text
Quote requirements should depend on urgency, amount, trade, and tenant impact.
```

### 4. Hidden Related-Party Economics Are A Trust Killer

The owner concern is not only "PM charges too much." It is "I cannot tell whether the PM, vendor, spouse-owned company, in-house maintenance arm, or contractor kickback is quietly shaping the repair cost."

### 5. Fee Stack Beats Headline Fee

Owners compare 8%, 10%, or flat monthly fees, but the real cost is the full annual stack:

- management fee
- leasing fee
- renewal fee
- reserve
- markup
- quote fee
- home warranty handling fee
- onboarding/admin fees
- termination/transfer fees
- vacancy fees
- inspection fees

### 6. Communication Is The Umbrella Complaint

Repair cost, approval, hidden fees, vacancy updates, and owner anxiety all collapse into communication when the PM does not proactively explain what happened.

## Good Blog/Post Anecdotes

Public-safe, reusable anecdotes:

- Owners are not always mad about paying a markup. They are mad when they discover the markup after the repair.
- A low management fee can be a decoy if repair markups, renewal fees, quote fees, and termination fees carry the real economics.
- Three quotes sound responsible until the tenant has no hot water and the best contractors stop bidding because they never win.
- A PM can be right that maintenance coordination is real labor, and an owner can be right that percentage markups create bad incentives.
- The cleanest answer is not "no markups." It is "show me the rule, show me the invoice, show me the approval, show me the proof."
