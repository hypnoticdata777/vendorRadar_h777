
- Is this vendor legally/operationally allowed to do the work?
- What insurance and licensing proof exists?
- Is the owner or PM protected if something goes wrong?

- Who has authority to hire, supervise, and pay the contractor?
- Is the lower bid truly comparable?

## Post-By-Post Notes

### 1. Certificate of Insurance for contractors

Evidence value: Extremely high.

This thread directly asks whether a landlord should require a COI for even small renovation work.

Key signals:

- The technical answer from multiple commenters: collect license, liability insurance, and workers comp proof.
- The practical answer: risk level matters. Painting may not carry the same risk as electrical, gas, plumbing, roof, structural, or other high-damage/high-injury work.
- Some commenters admitted they historically skipped license/insurance collection but now view it as too risky.
- Contractor injury exposure is a major concern.
- Insurance carriers may expect owners to keep current certificates for contractors.
- Some contractors can automatically renew/send certificates each year.
- Additional insured status is stronger than merely holding a certificate, but harder to enforce for smaller jobs.
- A certificate is proof that coverage exists; it is not always the same as being properly added to the policy.
- For GC/subcontractor chains, owners may want both GC and subcontractors to carry proper coverage and additional insured endorsements.

Interpretation:

Vendor compliance is risk-tiered. "Always get the docs" is safest, but real-world execution varies by job size and risk. The product needs to support both best-practice compliance and pragmatic workflows.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should model compliance by job risk:

- low-risk cosmetic
- moderate repair
- licensed trade
- high-damage trade
- life-safety
- structural
- roof/exterior/height risk
- GC/subcontractor chain

### 2. Working with vendors and contractors

Evidence value: Very high.

This older PM-side thread is directly aligned with VendorRadar. A property management company managing hundreds of properties asks how others collect license and insurance information for vendors.

Key signals:

- A PM company wants license and insurance information for vendors across many managed properties.
- Vendor types include carpet cleaning, general maintenance, electricians, and similar service providers.
- The motivating risk: a cheaper vendor without insurance gets hurt or causes damage, creating liability for PM and owner.
- One commenter described verifying subcontractor license, bond, and insurance information and checking online to confirm license currency.
- Another described a vendor who was licensed/insured on first use but no longer compliant two years later, leading to loss when credentials were not rechecked.
- The PM describes requiring current insurance policy/certificate and a vendor compliance agreement.
- Vendor compliance agreement topics included required response time, show time, scope control, and instructions not to give tenants off-scope opinions.
- The PM views vendors onsite as an extension of the management company.

Interpretation:

Compliance freshness matters. A vendor that was valid when first used may become invalid later. Vendor behavior at the property also matters: response time, show time, tenant communication, and staying within scope are part of compliance with PM operating standards.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should support vendor agreements as operational controls, not only legal files:

- response SLA
- arrival/show-time expectations
- scope-of-ticket boundary
- tenant communication rules
- proof/documentation rules
- insurance/license requirements
- renewal/check cadence
- last verified date

### 3. PM maintenance and repairs

Evidence value: High as duplicate/continuity.

This thread was already integrated in BiggerPockets Search 1, but it remains relevant here because it asks owners to evaluate whether PM maintenance vendors are licensed, bonded, insured, covered by workers comp, available 24/7, and overseen for quality/code/cost.

Key signals:

- Owners should ask whether maintenance is handled by in-house crew or preferred vendors.
- If in-house, owners should ask about workers comp, license, bonding, insurance, and emergency availability.
- If external, owners should ask whether contractors carry workers comp and proper licensing/insurance.
- PMs should oversee contractor work for quality, code compliance, and cost effectiveness.
- Contractor entry into occupied units should follow an established policy.
- Itemized statements and receipts create transparency.
- Tenant-performed repairs and tenant-hired handymen create liability/quality risk.

Interpretation:

Vendor compliance is part of choosing a property manager. Owners are not only selecting the PM; they are indirectly selecting the PM's vendor risk system.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar can become a proof layer PMs use to show owners that vendor risk is managed.

### 4. Advice on managing your property manager

Evidence value: Extremely high.

This thread links cost trust, owner-sourced competing bids, and vendor qualification.

Key signals:

- Owner received a high quote from the PM for a major repair and found a much lower independent quote.
- Commenters advised asking whether the PM adds maintenance fees and requesting invoices directly from vendors.
- Multiple commenters stressed that a lower bid may not be comparable if the alternative contractor is unlicensed, uninsured, unpermitted, lower quality, or using a different scope/materials.
- Large jobs should generally receive more than one quote.
- Getting multiple bids can hurt vendor relationships if vendors feel they are being used for free pricing.
- One suggestion was to pay for bids so contractors do not feel their time is wasted.
- Owners can use their own vendors, but the vendor should be licensed and insured.
- Contractor track record matters: showing up, skilled workers, safe work, tenant/property respect, and standing behind the work.
- For capital projects, owners may be better off working directly with a vendor outside the PM or requesting additional quotes through the PM.

Interpretation:

Bid comparison requires scope normalization. The $3,200 quote and the $8,500 quote may be a ripoff, or they may represent different insurance, urgency, materials, permits, safety, and scope. Owners need a way to compare bids honestly.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should support bid-comparison metadata:

- licensed/insured status
- workers comp status
- permit included
- material grade
- scope detail
- urgency premium
- warranty
- tenant access/safety plan
- PM markup
- vendor performance history

### 5. Property manager vendors are expensive

Evidence value: Very high.

This thread shows the cost/coverage tradeoff of PM-selected vendors versus owner-sourced vendors.

Key signals:

- Out-of-state owner found PM's vendor significantly more expensive than other estimates.
- Some investors advise sourcing their own contractor for major repairs while letting the PM handle minor work.
- PMs may add a percentage to vendor bills to compensate for repair management.
- Some owners question the value of PM fees if they still need to collect bids and manage repairs themselves.
- PM-side counterpoint: owner-selected bargain vendors may be slow, unavailable, hard to communicate with, or unable to serve tenant urgency.
- Tenant frustration and delay costs matter.
- The PM's known responsive vendor may cost more but resolve the issue faster.
- The owner must decide whether they are paying the PM to save time or whether they want to self-manage vendor selection.

Interpretation:

Vendor cost should be evaluated against response time, tenant impact, availability, reliability, and PM time. A cheaper vendor who cannot arrive for two weeks may be a bad operational choice.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should make tradeoffs visible:

- cost estimate
- expected response time
- tenant impact
- vendor availability
- owner-selected vs PM-selected
- PM effort required
- risk/compliance status

### 6. Hiring contractors for someone else's home

Evidence value: High as authority/liability evidence.

This thread asks whether someone can build a business brokering contractors for homeowners, similar to property management.

Key signals:

- Property managers act as agents of the owner through the property management agreement.
- State law may require a real estate license for property management and a contractor license for construction/renovation involvement.
- Once a third party hires or coordinates contractors, liability enters the picture.
- Suggested insurance includes general liability and professional liability/errors and omissions.
- Agreements may need hold-harmless terms, additional insured status, waiver of subrogation, primary/non-contributory coverage, indemnification, cancellation notices, and proof of liability/auto/workers comp.
- A PM commenter noted that PM infrastructure is built around repeat managed properties, not one-time homeowner jobs; one-time customer contractor brokering has heavy admin and thin margins.

Interpretation:

Vendor coordination is not just "matching." The moment a business coordinates contractor work for someone else's property, agency, licensing, liability, insurance, payment, and contract design matter.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should avoid positioning itself as an unstructured contractor broker without considering authority and liability. Safer early positioning is internal/vendor-record intelligence for PM teams, not taking legal responsibility for hiring.

### 7. GC license / owner project manager arrangement

Evidence value: Medium to high as adjacent authority evidence.

This thread is not property-management maintenance, but it clarifies authority and risk around GC-of-record arrangements.

Key signals:

- Using a GC's license while the owner/project manager hires subcontractors raised immediate concern.
- Commenters questioned legality, permit responsibility, insurance responsibility, who meets inspectors, and who holds prime contractor responsibility.
- Owner-builder rules may not apply to investment properties in many places.
- Licensed subcontractors, insurance, and clear contracting structure are central.
- A legitimate arrangement would need careful contracts and clear responsibility.

Interpretation:

Authority must match responsibility. A party cannot casually borrow a license, hire subs, and assume risk is handled. This reinforces why PM vendor workflows need clear roles.

VendorRadar implication:

VendorRadar should track who is responsible for each job:

- owner-directed
- PM-directed
- GC-directed
- subcontractor under GC
- vendor hired directly by owner
- vendor hired by PM as owner agent

## Cross-Thread Patterns

### 1. Compliance Is Fresh, Not Permanent

A vendor can be licensed and insured today but not next year. Search 4 gives a non-Reddit validation of COI/license recheck needs.

### 2. Scope Control Is Part Of Vendor Compliance

Vendors are not just required to be insured. They may also be required to respond within a certain time, arrive within a window, stay within the work-ticket scope, and avoid giving tenants off-scope opinions.

### 3. Cheap Bids Need Qualification Context

Owners compare dollar amounts, but the actual comparison should include license, insurance, workers comp, permits, material quality, warranty, urgency, tenant access, and vendor reliability.

### 4. Owner-Selected Vendors Are Not Automatically Better

Owners may save money with their own vendors, especially on large repairs, but PMs worry about delays, communication burden, tenant dissatisfaction, and vendor reliability.

### 5. Contractor Coordination Creates Liability

If a person or company hires contractors for someone else's property, they may be acting as agent, project manager, GC, or broker. That has licensing, insurance, and contract implications.

### 6. Vendor Compliance Is Also PM Sales Evidence

Owners ask whether PMs use licensed, bonded, insured vendors and how they oversee quality, code compliance, and cost effectiveness. PMs can use clean vendor compliance records as trust proof.
